# Blog 1 
What is Data Science to Me?

Why Model Social Topics and People?

One example that's always stuck with me is traffic jams that seem to appear for no clear reason, sometimes called phantom traffic jams. No single driver is really doing anything wrong. Someone brakes a little, the person behind them reacts a little later, and that small delay keeps moving backward through the line until it can turn into a complete stop. What I find interesting about this is that you can't really understand the traffic jam by looking at just one driver's decision. You have to look at how everyone's decisions interact.

I think a lot of social phenomena work in a similar way. Things like segregation patterns, market crashes, or sudden changes in public opinion aren't necessarily caused by one person's decision. Lots of people make individual choices, and those choices can add up to something that nobody actually intended. That's one reason I think modeling social topics matters. It gives us a way to look for patterns that aren't obvious when we're only looking at individuals. It also lets us test ideas about how those patterns develop without having to experiment on people's actual lives.

Responsibilities With Data About People

If I were building a model using someone's neighborhood, income, or online activity, one thing I'd be especially concerned about is what those variables might be standing in for. A variable might seem neutral but still be connected to race, class, or other characteristics that shouldn't be determining someone's outcome. Even if a model doesn't directly use one of those categories, it could still end up making decisions based on a related variable.

There's also a difference between getting a model wrong in theory and getting it wrong when the result affects a real person. If someone used a model to deny a person a loan or label them as a risk, that person might not even know a model was involved. They might also have no way to understand or challenge the decision. I think people whose data is being used should at least have some idea of how it is being used and how it might affect them. Otherwise, it becomes difficult to hold anyone accountable when something goes wrong.

What I’m Hoping to Understand

What I want to understand better is why people's collective behavior can be so difficult to predict, even when we have a lot of data about them. The traffic-jam example is interesting to me for that reason. The individual decisions can make sense while the overall result still ends up being surprising. 

I'd also like to get better at recognizing when a model is making assumptions that don't really fit how people behave. Social behavior seems a lot harder to reduce to a few variables than some other things we might model. I want to be able to notice that problem before a model's results turn into a decision that has a real effect on someone's life.
